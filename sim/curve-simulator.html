Why not always pick Curve Long?

Because the explosive upside is rented, not owned. At p = 2 and 80% implied volatility a Curve Long loses about half its size in a year if NEAR goes nowhere, and a 10% drop costs 19%. The card shows the price NEAR must beat just to break even. Like buying options, it pays big now and then and loses a little most of the time. Curve Short is the mirror image.

Why the PnL lines go flat

The pool is the ceiling. When long claims reach the whole pool, Curve Short is wiped out, longs take the pool, and the claim above it is written off. Nobody is liquidated one by one. From that moment new shorts can join at the current price, with the full rally ahead of them as room. Utilization funding makes that very attractive: near 100% longs pay over 20× the fair rate.

Is there leverage?

Nobody borrows and nobody can be liquidated. Curve Long carries built-in leverage of p: at p = 2 a 1% move changes its value by about 2%, but it can only shrink toward zero. Curve Short is a fully collateralized short of size p × the long claims. There are no 10× shorts; the most anyone can lose is what they deposited.

Three directions, two volatility bets

Curve Long is bullish and long volatility. Curve Short is bearish and short volatility. Add a hedge and the direction disappears: Long + short NEAR wins on big moves either way, and Short + long NEAR is the sideways "Earn" position that collects funding when the market is quiet.

How the numbers are made

Curve units are worth NF × (P / P₀)p. NF decays at p(p−1)/2 × σ² per year; that decay is the funding. Curve Short owns the pool minus the long claims. Hedges are rebalanced once a day at p × the current long value. Hedge trading costs and fees on the external market are left out.
