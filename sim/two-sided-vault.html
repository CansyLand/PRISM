Two products, one pool

Retail buys Curve Long, a lottery ticket on BTC that can't be liquidated. The only short is a Hedged Vault: it sells Curve Short and holds BTC to cancel the price exposure, so what's left is the funding the longs pay. This page runs that setup on real BTC prices and on random paths, and checks whether the vault beats ETH staking.

  • Steep curves are the vault's income and retail's lottery. At 200 players and +25% premium, raising the cap from p = 4 to p = 10 lifts the vault from 3.4% to 9.2% and the chance of 2× from 2.5% to 6.3%, but lowers payback from $0.94 to $0.86 and doubles the vault's drawdown (−2.2%).

  • Payback below $1 is the price of the ticket. A casino slot pays back about $0.90 to $0.95 and a state lottery about $0.50. Around $0.90 is a fair target for a long that can't be liquidated and can't lose more than the ticket.

Does the two-product setup work?

How the vault works, step by step

A long claim is what the pool owes a Curve Long right now: ticket × (BTC now ÷ BTC at entry)p, minus funding paid so far. A $100 long at p = 3 is owed $133 after a 10% rally (1.1³ = 1.331) and $73 after a 10% drop. The pool pays every claim out of the same USDC, and whatever is left belongs to the short side.

Why p × claims of BTC. When BTC moves 1%, a claim at curvature p moves about p%. A $1,000 claim at p = 10 grows by about $100 for a 1% rally, the same as holding $10,000 of BTC. So the vault cancels that move by holding $10,000 of BTC, ten times the claim. At p = 2 it needs only $2,000. This is why steep curves tie up the most capital.

Daily rebalancing. The amount needed changes as BTC moves: after a 10% rally that $1,000 claim at p = 10 is worth about $2,594, so the vault now needs about $25,900 of BTC, not $10,000. Once a day the keeper reads the oracle, computes p × claims for every curve, and buys or sells BTC to match. It always buys after a rise and sells after a fall. That small loss on every move is the cost of being short convexity, and the funding has to cover it.

Deposits in USD only. A depositor sends USDC (or any token through NEAR Intents, swapped to USDC). The vault itself splits the money: part goes into the pool as Curve Short, part buys BTC for the hedge. Depositors own vault shares, not BTC. When BTC rises, the BTC in the vault gains about what the short side loses, so the share price stays roughly flat and moves up by the funding earned. The depositor never sees the BTC leg.

Capital and yield find a balance. Yield ≈ net spread × claims ÷ vault size. A small vault next to many players earns a lot, which draws in deposits; deposits grow the vault, and the same funding is split over more capital, so yield falls until it is just above what depositors can get elsewhere. Switch on "Capital follows yield" to watch this: the vault settles at a size set by retail activity, not by its starting capital. A small starting fund is fine; it starts with a high yield.

Why the hedge isn't free

Curve Short holds −(claims), and claims grow like Pp. The vault cancels the first-order move by holding p × claims worth of BTC, rebalanced daily. What's left each day is:

vault PnL per day ≈ claims × p(p−1)/2 × (funding variance × multiplier − realized variance) × dt

So the vault earns only if funding is priced above the volatility that actually happens. Two consequences: the funding multiplier must stay at or above 1 even at low utilization (today's contract uses 0.5 + u, which pays shorts less than fair below 50%), and funding should follow realized volatility, not a fixed setting. The premium slider on the right is that markup.

The second limit is size: a vault can only earn on long claims that exist. Yield on vault capital ≈ net spread × claims ÷ vault capital. More players or a smaller vault raise it, and utilization rises with it, which raises funding again.
